Ten Thousand Years of Peoples

The census rolls of the Mittelland go back further than any living scholar can remember — ten millennia of births, deaths, and migrations, carved into stone in the oldest archives and scrawled in ledgers in the newest.

The oldest entries are little more than tally marks scratched onto slate by the Elven scribes of the First Age. They counted only themselves at first, and then — grudgingly, when trade demanded it — the Dwarves who came down from the Iron Peaks with their wagons of ore and salt.

It took another two thousand years before the Mittelland's census acknowledged the existence of the Orcs at all. For centuries the ledgers list them under the catch-all heading «beasts of the border», a clerical slight that modern historians still argue about in the academies of Thal and Lindenberg.

The Humans arrived late, as Humans always do. By the time their first villages appeared on the southern coast, the Mittelland was already old, already charted, already sure of itself. The scribes gave them their own column on the rolls with what one chronicler called «audible reluctance».

And the Halflings, last of the five, slipped into the records almost by accident — listed first as tenants on Elven estates, then as a people in their own right, then finally, in the Fourth Age, as the fastest-growing community in the lowland shires.

What follows is a walk through the chronicle itself. Scroll on to see how the five peoples — Elves, Dwarves, Orcs, Humans, and Halflings — rose, fell, and reshuffled themselves across the great span of recorded time. And how differently things might have gone.

History is the easier half of the problem. The harder half is what comes next — the ten thousand years that have not yet happened, and the assumptions a Prognosticator must make to say anything about them at all.

What the rolls can — and cannot — tell us

Ten thousand years of counting produces a vivid picture, but a flat one. Behind every curve is a migration, a war, a plague the chroniclers did not see fit to name — and every alternative timeline above is a story about a choice that was or was not made.

The Royal Academy of Prognostication has, at last count, eleven working models of the next ten millennia, and no two of them agree on anything more than the weather in year 2000. The four we have shown here are merely the ones that fit on a single page.

It is tempting to treat the chronicle as destiny — to read the great Human curve as inevitable, the Elven decline as natural law. But a census is descriptive, not prescriptive. It tells you what happened. It never tells you what had to.

The next time a bard claims the Elves are dying, remember: there are more Elves alive today than there were Humans at the founding of the Mittelland. The peoples change not because they vanish, but because the world around them keeps making more of itself.

And the next time a Prognosticator claims to know the shape of the Sixth Age, remember the Scribes of Thal. They kept the most careful rolls in the Mittelland, and still — still — they missed the Orc Surge, the Plague of the Fourth Age, and the founding of every Human city on the southern coast.

The chronicle is what we have. It is never, quite, all that there is.
